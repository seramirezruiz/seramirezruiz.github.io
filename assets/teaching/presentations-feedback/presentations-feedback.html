Where to Find Research Problems

What counts as a “research problem”?

A research problem is a puzzle about how (in our case) institutions, parties, or political behavior actually work… not just a topic.

“I’m interested in populism” or “I study political parties” isn’t a question yet. You need something unexplored, overlooked, or unresolved about it.

  • IMO: Good problems combine two things: a clearly stated puzzle, and a reason it matters — theoretically (it advances a debate) and/or practically.
  • Disciplinary note: There might be some differences across disciplines around the acceptable framing of puzzles. Some expect an explicit “so what”, while others might be more accepting of puzzles framed around social structure, meaning, or process, without needing a direct payoff. Both are legitimate, but know which conversation you’re entering.

Some places real research problems come from

An empirical anomaly
Something the dominant theory says shouldn't happen... but does.
A theoretical contradiction
Two established theories predict opposite outcomes in a case you can actually observe.
An untested scope condition
A finding proven in one setting (advanced democracies, one country) that's never been checked elsewhere.
A newly usable data source
Administrative records, digitized archives, text, audio, video... that make an old question answerable for the first time.
A live puzzle in the world
Something happening right now that existing frameworks have difficulty explaining.
Your own recurring "wait, why?" (i.e., vibes)
A moment from your reading or experience you genuinely can't let go of.

Where problems come from at the macro level

  • Shared disciplinary debates: party system fragmentation and dealignment, institutional persistence vs. change, the social bases of political behavior, the link between social structure and political identity.
  • Big questions → incremental application cycles: Major shifts (behavioral revolution, historical institutionalism, relational sociology, causal inference, computational methods, AI) open waves of new applications and lenses to study ‘big questions’.
  • External priorities: Funders, policy relevance, and current events (democratic backsliding, polarization, misinformation, populist mobilization) pull research agendas in our fields. Good for relevance and fundability, but risks everyone converging on the same ‘trendy’ topic.

Not every gap is a problem

A gap existing is not, by itself, a real problem. Plenty of gaps are gaps for a reason — before you commit a proposal to one, some things to keep in mind:

  • Stakes: would studying this lead to actual ‘learning’ of some form — about this case, this population, this institution? (Confirming a known pattern holds somewhere new is still valuable; it doesn’t have to overturn anything.)
  • Traction: could you actually gather evidence that would let you be wrong about it?
  • Durability: will this still feel worth answering in year three, once the initial excitement has worn off?

On “scouting” and finding your own puzzle

IME our sense of the “real” question sharpens as we read, talk, and work. You start out interested in “party decline” and realize you’re actually drawn to how party organizations lose their grip on collective identity.

Practical tip: keep a running list of 10–20 loose questions that genuinely interest you. Read new work against that list, and notice when something clicks.

The sharpening doesn’t happen in one sitting… it happens gradually. This workshop is one more go at it: what you freewrote this morning, what you drafted into your proposal, what you say out loud later today or tomorrow, and what you revise afterward are the same puzzle getting sharper each time.

Some thoughts for aspiring researchers (and Ph.D. applicants)

  • There’s no single correct way to find a dissertation topic. Deliberate reading, reactive problem-solving, proactive scouting, talking to people… topics come in different forms.
  • A good research problem needs both a genuine puzzle and a clear stake. Invest some real time here before jumping to the specifics of individual papers.
  • Finding the problem is the hard, often underrated first step. How you go about answering it (i.e., case selection, data, method) is a separate, later challenge.

You’re not starting from zero… being here means you already convinced a committee you have a version of a puzzle worth pursuing. The work this week isn’t finding one from nothing; it’s recognizing it clearly enough to sharpen it under some scrutiny.

What Makes a Strong Proposal

You’ve spent months (or years) thinking about the details in your idea.
Whoever reads your proposal has spent none.

Write for someone in your discipline… not in your literature.

Every proposal should answer (at least) three questions

  • What is your central question or puzzle?
  • Why should anybody care… and specifically, who?
  • How will you answer it?

In writing, these aren’t three separate sections. They should read cohesively as one argument: the puzzle only matters because of the why, and the how only makes sense once the reader already believes the why.

Research question… a topic is not a question

Topic dressed as a question

“How has democratic backsliding changed party competition?”

An actual puzzle

“Under what conditions do incumbents dismantle checks on their own power without triggering an electoral backlash?”

What, in your view, actually differentiates these two?

Positioning in the literature

A gap existing is not, by itself, a real problem. Plenty of gaps are gaps for a reason — before you commit a proposal to one — you have to argue this one is.

Some ways to make that case:

  • The tools exist but nobody has used them
  • The claim has only been tested in a narrow slice
  • Two camps quietly assume different things
  • The world keeps producing cases the dominant account can’t explain

Mechanisms — “why,” not just “that”

A correlation between two macro trends is a pattern, not an explanation. A strong proposal (in most cases) shows the mechanism connecting them to back the assumptions up.

Macro condition
Declining social trust
→
Individual belief
"Mainstream parties won't represent me"
→
Individual behavior
Votes for an outsider/niche party
→
Macro outcome
Party system fragmentation

Naming some model of the world (data generation process) — even briefly — is usually worth more to a reader than another page of description.

Methodology — problem-driven, not method-driven

  • The method follows from the question. If you can name your method before you can state your question precisely, something’s backwards.
  • Justify case selection explicitly: why this data form, country, party, or time span — and if it’s comparative, why these cases and not others?

If you can’t say why this method answers your specific question better than an obvious alternative, expect a reviewer to ask.

Feasibility — will you actually get this done?

Feasibility isn’t a formality at the end of the proposal — it’s often what separates a strong (fundable) project from an appealing idea.

It’s not about having everything figured out. It’s about signals that show a reader this isn’t just aspirational:

Specificity over gesture
Name the exact source, you intend to use — or, if you'd be collecting new data yourself, a concrete signal that you're the person who can actually do it (access, language, network, prior fieldwork).
(If possible) evidence of a first step already taken
Something that shows the plan has already survived contact with reality.
A friction point you've priced in
Response rates, translation, access negotiations, ethics review timelines — name the realistic obstacle. Leaving it out reads as not having thought about it.
A fallback
What you'd still be able to answer if your first-choice data or access falls through.

So far, everything we’ve covered — the question, the gap, the design —
would matter in any research proposal.

(If you are thinking about a PhD…) This one has to convince someone to supervise you, specifically, for multiple years.

Fit with program and supervisor

“I want to work at the EUI (or with Professor X) because they’re well known” is not fit. Fit runs in both directions — what they can offer you, and what you bring to them.

What they offer you

  • Methodological training or data infrastructure you’d actually use.
  • Mentorship in the specific debate you’re entering, not just the subfield label.
  • A research culture that matches how you want to work.

What you bring them

  • Some expertise that extends what the department or research group can study.
  • Some technical skill that isn’t already covered in-house
  • Some signal that you’ll be a colleague who adds to the room, not just a project to supervise.

Most times, it helps to name ‘names’ and say precisely what in their work your project speaks to — and what, specifically, you’d add to it.

Timeline — what four years actually looks like

Year 1
Coursework, refine the research question, pilot data access/collection
Year 2
Main data collection or fieldwork
Year 3
Analysis and drafts, research stays
Year 4
Writing, revising, job market

A vague timeline signals you haven’t thought about feasibility. A realistic one is itself a feasibility argument.

Putting it together

Six lenses. This is what we will listen for while you all present.

Research question

Literature positioning
Shows what's missing, not just what already exists.
Methodology
Justified by the question, not assumed.
Feasibility
Data you can actually get, on a timeline you can defend.
Fit*
Specific to institutional strenghts or potential supervisors' work (not generic praise).
Timeline*
A realistic year-by-year path, not a vague four-year gesture.
* May not come up in the 7–9 minute pitch itself, but they still matter for the written proposal.
